# Positioning

## What it is

The Oolio Product OS is the Product team's way of working, written down as skills an assistant
can run against the tools the team already uses. Thirty-two of them today, arranged across a
lifecycle that runs from the first signal to the measured outcome.

It is not a framework, a methodology, or a diagram of intent. It is the thing itself. Somebody
installs it and the next PRD they write is written with it.

## The names, and which is which

**Pixie Dust Industries** is the house. **Product OS** is what it ships.

- The house name is on the door: the header lockup, the footer, the link preview, a title
  slide. The product name is what a page is about: the tag beside the headline, the sentence
  under it, the thing somebody installs.
- **"Industries", not "Services".** Industries buys the whole print-shop vocabulary — drums,
  passes, plates, press checks, run numbers — and the joke lands: a factory that manufactures
  pixie dust. It also suits a thing that ships versioned by commit. Services reads
  consultancy, someone you hire by the day, and a service does not have drums.
- Not "the OS" in anything a person outside the team will read. Inside the team it is fine.
- Not "PDI", "ProductOS", "Product-OS", or any acronym. An acronym is a thing you have to be
  taught; the point of a name is that nobody has to be.
- **The addresses do not move.** The plugin is `oolio-pm`, the repository is
  `niel-cody/oolio-product-os`, the site is `oolio-product-os.vercel.app`. Those are package
  ids and URLs, not the brand, and every installed reference and skill namespace depends on
  them. Renaming any of them would break every install for no reader's benefit.

## The line

> The product process, written down and running.

It is the whole positioning in seven words, and it earns its keep by being falsifiable: anybody
can check whether it is true, and today it is. Use it in link previews, on the front door, and
at the top of a deck. Do not decorate it, extend it, or set it as a question.

The supporting sentence, where there is room for one:

> Not a diagram of how we intend to work. The thing itself.

## The thesis

> The loop closes on a person.

This is the idea the whole identity is built on, and it is why the mark is a ring with a break
in it and why the third drum is reserved for the review gates. The OS automates the tax on
product management. It does not automate the judgement, and the brand says so before a word is
read.

Use the thesis when explaining the mark or the colour. It is not a tagline. Do not put it on a
t-shirt.

## Who it is for

| Audience | What they need from the brand | Where they meet it |
|---|---|---|
| **An Oolio PM** | To believe the standard is real, and that installing it is five minutes | The site, the install block, the skill list |
| **An Oolio exec** | To see that the team's discipline does not depend on who is having a good week | A deck, a Steering pack, the map |
| **The rest of Oolio** | To understand what Product actually does, in one screen | The Confluence front door, a link preview in Slack |
| **A candidate or a peer** | To find a team that has thought hard about its craft | The site, if it is ever shown outside |

The first audience is the one the brand is built for. Everything else is downstream of a PM
finding it credible.

## The five principles

These are the tests. A design decision that fails one of them is wrong, however good it looks.

**1. The evidence is the argument.**
Every number on a surface is generated from the thing it describes. The skill count, the stage
count, the changelog. No hand-typed figures anywhere, because a brand whose facts drift stops
being believed at exactly the moment it matters.

**2. The third drum is a person.**
Sun Yellow means a human decides here. It is the smallest ink budget on the sheet and it is
spent on the review gates and nothing else. As the only warm thing on the page it makes "a
person signs off anything that counts" the loudest mark on the map, which is what the page
claims in words already.

**3. The constraint is the design.**
Three drums, one ink per pass, and paper that shows through. The look holds together because
the palette cannot grow, not because somebody exercised restraint. If a page needs a fourth
colour, it needs an overprint, and the press decides what that is.

**4. It reads at 400 and at 4.**
The mark works at sixteen pixels. The palette clears AA on every surface it is used on. The
type is legible at nine pixels because that is where most of it lives. Craft that only survives
the hero shot is decoration.

**5. Nothing implies more than is true.**
No stock imagery of people who do not exist. No metrics we have not measured. No "AI-powered".
The OS is unusual enough described accurately.

## Positioning against the obvious neighbours

| | They look like | We look like |
|---|---|---|
| Developer tools | Violet on near-black, geometric sans, gradient mesh | Three inks on uncoated stock, an art-press display face, no gradients at all |
| Consultancy decks | Blue, photography, capability wheels | Generated diagrams of a real system |
| Notion and Confluence templates | Emoji, pastel callouts | Typography and a keyline |

The differentiator is not the palette. It is that the artefacts are generated from a running
system, and the brand's job is to make that legible in the first two seconds. The press makes
the same point twice: a Riso is a machine that produces things one pass at a time and shows
its own registration errors, which is a fair description of what the Product OS does.
